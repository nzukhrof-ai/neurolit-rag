Abstract
The analysis and treatment of brain tumors are among the most difficult medical condi­
tions. Brain tumors must be detected accurately and promptly to improve patient outcomes
and plan effective treatments. Recently used advanced technologies such as artificial intel­
ligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) have increased interest in applying AI to detect
brain tumors. However, concerns have emerged regarding the reliability and transparency
of AI models in medical settings, as their decision-making processes are often opaque and
difficult to interpret. This research is unique in its focus on explainability in AI-based brain
tumor detection, prioritizing confidence, safety, and clinical adoption over mere accuracy.
It gives a thorough overview of XAI methodologies, problems, and uses, linking scientific
advances to the needs of real-world healthcare. XAI is a sub-section of artificial intel­
ligence that seeks to solve this problem by offering understandable and straightforward
and providing explanations for the choices made by AI representations. Applications such
as healthcare, where the interpretability of AI models is essential for guaranteeing pa­
tient safety and fostering confidence between medical professionals and AI systems, have
seen the introduction of XAI-based procedures. This paper reviews recent advancements
in XAI-based brain tumor detection, focusing on methods that provide justifications for
AI model predictions. The study highlights the advantages of XAI in improving patient
outcomes and supporting medical decision-making. The findings reveal that ResNet 18
performed better, with 94% training accuracy, 96.86% testing accuracy, low loss (0.012),
and a rapid time (∼6s). ResNet 50 was a little slower (∼13s) but stable, with 92.86%
test accuracy. DenseNet121 (Adam W) achieved the highest accuracy at 97.71%, but it
was not consistent across all optimizers. ViT-GRU also got 97% accuracy with very little
loss (0.008), although it took a long time to compute (around 49 s). On the other hand,
VGG models (around 94% test accuracy) and MobileNetV2 (loss up to 6.024) were less
reliable, even though they trained faster. Additionally, it explores various opportunities,
challenges, and clinical applications. Based on these findings, this research offers a com­
prehensive analysis of XAI-based brain tumor detection and encourages further investiga­
tion in specific areas.

1  Introduction
Brain tumors are among the most challenging conditions to diagnose and treat because of
their complexity and the wide range of symptoms that patients may experience. Although it
has been demonstrated that artificial intelligence improves the accuracy and usefulness of
brain tumor discovery, questions have been raised regarding the safety and dependability
of AI algorithms in the health field due to their complexity and difficulty in understanding.
To address this challenge, XAI has emerged, providing succinct and comprehensible clari­
fications for the choices made by AI models. The medical community and the AI system
can work together more effectively and with greater accountability when clinicians can
comprehend the underlying causes of the AI’s predictions thanks to XAI techniques. This
study summarizes the latest advancements in XAI-based brain tumor detection, including
different methods for producing explanations, and talks about the opportunities and difficul­
ties of applying XAI in clinical settings. Similar to other body organs, the brain can develop
issues, including growths called brain tumors (Yurtsever et al. 2024). These tumors develop
from abnormal cell growth in various regions of the brain, potentially leading to severe
neurological complications and a significant decline in the patient’s quality of life. Most
people concur that brain tumors rank among the deadliest diseases worldwide, and they
significantly impact death rates across all age groups (Gyamfi et al. 2022). In terms of medi­
cal diagnosis and treatment, this condition presents difficulties. These tumors can appear as
pituitary, glioma, meningioma, or in cases where there is no tumor at all.
Due to the composite structure of the brain, the nearly 120 different types of tumors
that have been discovered so far are inspiring to identify due to their varied features and
sizes. Edema, necrotic centers, and tumorous tissues are examples of tumorous areas that
must be precisely identified and segmented to aid in diagnosis and action planning. It is
unbearable to overstate the status of automatically identifying and categorizing medical
images, especially when diagnosing brain tumors (Ahmed et al. 2024; Raghavendra et al.
2023). For brain tumors to be treated more rapidly and give patients better outcomes, they
must be detected and categorized early. This is followed by the same MID. It is the conven­
tional method of finding brain tumors. PET, CT, and MRI scans are examples of medical
imaging methods that are essential for identifying brain tumors. The most famous system
for examining the brain’s structures is MRI. Because MRI is extensively used and real in
investigating brain structures, it was selected as the preferred modality for our investigation
study. Although MRI is capable of modeling regions of interest, the differences in strength
levels brought about by different machine settings make it difficult to differentiate cancers
(Abdusalomov et al. 2023). To this end, DL approaches are creating tools for brain cancer
classification and segmentation. They provide precise and reliable information about the
kind, location, and existence of tumors in computer-aided brain tumor diagnosis systems.
PRISMA is a set of rules that makes sure systematic reviews and meta-analyses are
clear, complete, and can be repeated. It combines a 27-item checklist and a flow diagram
to concisely explain study selection, methods, and results. The PRISMA framework is fre­
quently mandated by journals and funding agencies, and it has seen widespread adoption in
healthcare and artificial intelligence in medicine. Its main goals are to increase the quality
of reviews while decreasing bias.

3  Algorithms for detection/classification of brain tumor
AI technique combines ML and DL with revolutionized medical imaging. These approaches
are enabled through automated extraction of relevant features with medical descriptions
such as MRI and CT Scans. These all help to improve the accuracy and detection of brain
tumors. To help radiologists, make better clinical decisions, AI models are made to recog­
nize patterns in information that do not directly affect people’s judgment.
3.1  Machine learning algorithms
As a branch of AI and ML, it entails training models on a dataset to identify trends and
generate predictions. The following machine learning procedures have been used to notice
brain tumors, Brain tumors are classified using SVM, which uses features taken from imag­
ing data. One collaborative learning method for tasks involving regression and classifica­
tion is RF. A straightforward and efficient classification technique that has been used to
identify brain tumors is KNN (Maqsood et al. 2022). These algorithms rely on manually
extracted tumor area characteristics like texture, shape, and intensity. Decision trees operate
by dividing data according to naturally interpretable feature values. It is possible to visualize

3.2  Deep learning algorithms
Among DL techniques, CNNs have occurred as the most hopeful approach to brain tumor
detection. The need for physical feature extraction can be avoided by using CNNs to
mechanically learn hierarchical features from raw medical images. Deep learning’s capacity
to manage sizable datasets and extract complex patterns straight from image pixels is what
makes it so effective at detecting brain tumors (Rao et al. 2024). Gliomas, meningiomas,
and pituitary tumors are among the brain tumors that CNN-based models are very good at
identifying and categorizing (Vankdothu and Hameed 2022). CNNs are a key module of
contemporary diagnostic AI because of their capacity to generalize across various kinds
of brain tumor datasets. Pre-trained models, such as VGG16 and ResNet, are commonly
optimized for brain tumor classification tasks to minimize the need for massive amounts of
labeled data in transfer learning (Montaha et al. 2022).

Deep learning architectures interact differently with explainability tools, influencing the
nature and utility of generated explanations. CNNs, with their hierarchical spatial feature
maps, are well-suited for gradient-based approaches such as Grad-CAM, which highlight
discriminative regions in medical images. Transformer-based models, on the other hand,
inherently incorporate self-attention mechanisms, enabling attention visualization and attri­
bution methods (e.g., attention rollout, SHAP) to reveal feature dependencies across image
regions. Generative models, including VAEs and GANs, can be integrated with counterfac­
tual explanations to synthesize plausible alternative cases, thereby providing insight into
feature-level reasoning and decision boundaries. Together, these interactions demonstrate
that the choice of XAI technique should be closely aligned with the underlying model archi­
tecture to maximize both fidelity and clinical usability.
Transfer learning plays a crucial role in brain tumor classification by leveraging pre-
trained models on large datasets to improve accuracy and reduce training time on limited
medical data. Key points include:
Improves accuracy Transfer learning uses features learned from large, diverse datasets
(often non-medical) and fine-tunes them with brain tumor MRI data. This significantly
improves classification accuracy for tumors such as benign vs malignant or specific
tumor types like glioma, meningioma, and pituitary tumors, often reaching accuracy
above 98–99%.
Reduces data requirements By building on pre-trained deep neural networks (e.g.,
ResNet, VGG, MobileNet), transfer learning reduces the need for large labeled medical
image datasets, which are costly and difficult to obtain.
Enhances generalization Fine-tuning pre-trained models on diverse brain tumor data­
sets helps models generalize better to unseen samples and different imaging conditions.
Speeds up training Transfer learning accelerates model convergence, enabling faster
development and deployment of AI diagnostic tools.
Supports multi-class classification Transfer learning enables effective differentiation
among multiple tumor categories (e.g., glioma, meningioma, pituitary, no tumor) with
high precision and recall.

3.3  Explainable AI (XAI)
Explainable AI in brain tumor detection and classification aims to make the decision-mak­
ing process of complex AI models transparent and interpretable while maintaining high
accuracy. Traditional deep learning models, though powerful in identifying tumors from
MRI images, often operate as black boxes with limited explainability, making it difficult
for clinicians to trust and validate their predictions. XAI techniques, such as Grad-CAM,
SHAP, and LIME, are integrated with CNNs to highlight the most relevant tumor regions
and critical features in the images, providing visual and quantitative explanations for the
model’s decisions.
By focusing on abnormal brain tissues responsible for different tumor subtypes, XAI-
enhanced models improve classification performance and generalizability, achieving accu­
racy as high as 99% on known data and about 95% on unseen datasets. This combination
of interpretability and accuracy helps clinicians better understand, trust, and adopt AI tools
for diagnosis. Furthermore, XAI methods validate that the models do not rely on irrelevant
features or overfit the data, making them robust and reliable for real-world clinical applica­
tions. The use of XAI in this domain represents a significant advancement toward transpar­
ent, accountable, and effective medical AI systems for brain tumor diagnosis and treatment
support.
XAI techniques such as feature importance methods, SHAP, LIME, rule extraction,
and visualization approaches like activation maps–break down these complex models into
understandable components. They reveal which input features have the most influence,
explain individual predictions locally, and highlight the critical regions or factors contribut­
ing to decisions. This transparency fosters trust, accountability, and facilitates identification
of biases or errors that would otherwise go unnoticed in black-box systems.

Explainable AI addresses the black-box limitations of AI models by making their decision-
making processes more transparent, understandable, and interpretable for humans. Here’s
how XAI tackles these challenges:
Feature importance XAI techniques highlight which input features most influenced the
model’s decision, helping users understand what factors contributed to a specific predic­
tion.
Local and global explanations Methods like LIME and SHAP provide clear, human-
readable explanations for individual predictions (local) or for the model’s overall be­
havior (global). This helps users see why a model arrived at a certain output in specific
cases as well as in general patterns.
Visualization tools Techniques such as activation maps and attention mechanisms visu­
ally reveal what parts of an input (such as regions in a medical image) the model focuses
on, offering insight into the model’s reasoning.
Rule extraction and surrogate models XAI can use simpler, interpretable models (like
decision trees or rule-based systems) to approximate and explain the behavior of com­
plex black-box models in understandable terms.
Bias and error detection By making AI decisions transparent, XAI enables the identifi­
cation of model biases and errors, supporting regulatory compliance and increasing user
trust in critical fields such as healthcare and finance.

3.3.1  Literature review
The Table 3 summarizes recent research on the application of XAI in brain tumor detection

3.4  Medical imaging modalities for brain tumor detection
The following medical imaging modalities have primarily employed AI techniques to detect
brain tumors: MRI is the most popular graphical method for brain tumor detection. They
offer high-resolution pictures and can differentiate between various brain tissue types. MRI
scans can be used for AI models that can detect growth patterns that could be signs of a
tumor (Ullah et al. 2022). CT scans can still be used to find brain tumors, despite being less
detailed than MRIs. AI algorithms can improve CT image analysis, particularly when MRI
scans are not available in an emergency. Early tumor detection can be aided by PET scans,
which determine metabolic activity in the brain. AI can help interpret PET data to improve
tumor detection (Singh et al. 2020).

The use of AI-based analytic models with MRI scans raises complex moral, ethical, and

When applying AI systems in clinical decision-making, ethical challenges become espe­
cially significant in cases involving rare tumors. The consequences of this type of issue are
noted as overdiagnosis, false positives, and missed diagnoses. The AI model does not train
through rare cases and results are unable to identify rare tumors. This can result in delayed
or failed early-stage treatment. AI diagnoses are further complicated by inadequate or
unrepresentative data, which can negatively impact subsequent medical decisions, includ­
ing operations and treatments. Therefore, ethical oversight, along with awareness of the
model’s limitations, is essential to address these issues effectively. Ethical AI in healthcare
is designed to take responsibility for patient safety by minimizing risk and ensuring compli­
ance with legal requirements.
Ethical concerns in AI-based diagnostics can be resolved through a combination of regu­
latory, technical, and operational strategies:
Patient consent and transparency Patients must be clearly informed about how AI is
used in their diagnosis and treatment, what data is collected, and any potential risks.
Full transparency includes explaining how the AI system works and what role it plays

in medical decisions.
Bias and fairness Rigorous evaluation and validation of AI algorithms should be per­

all patient groups.
Accountability and liability Clearly defined legal frameworks should determine respon­
sibility in case of errors or harm caused by AI systems, potentially through joint liabil­
ity between developers, healthcare providers, and institutions. Developers must certify

Regulation and oversight Implementation of strict regulatory standards, independent

performance monitoring help ensure ethical compliance, safety, and effectiveness.
Continual improvement Regular updates to algorithms, ongoing research, and adapta­
